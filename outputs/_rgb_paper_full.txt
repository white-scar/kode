Abstract 
High-throughput phenotyping (HTP) has expanded the dimensionality of data in plant research; however, HTP has 
resulted in few novel biological discoveries to date. Field-based HTP (FHTP), using small unoccupied aerial vehicles 
(UAVs) equipped with imaging sensors, can be deployed routinely to monitor segregating plant population interactions 
with the environment under biologically meaningful conditions. Here, flowering dates and plant height, important phe-
nological fitness traits, were collected on 520 segregating maize recombinant inbred lines (RILs) in both irrigated and 
drought stress trials in 2018. Using UAV phenomic, single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) genomic, as well as com-
bined data, flowering times were predicted using several scenarios. Untested genotypes were predicted with 0.58, 
0.59, and 0.41 prediction ability for anthesis, silking, and terminal plant height, respectively, using genomic data, but 
prediction ability increased to 0.77, 0.76, and 0.58 when phenomic and genomic data were used together. Using the 
phenomic data in a genome-wide association study, a heat-related candidate gene (GRMZM2G083810; hsp18f) was 
discovered using temporal reflectance phenotypes belonging to flowering times (both irrigated and drought) trials 
where heat stress also peaked. Thus, a relationship between plants and abiotic stresses belonging to a specific time 
of growth was revealed only through use of temporal phenomic data. Overall, this study showed that (i) it is possible 
to predict complex traits using high dimensional phenomic data between different environments, and (ii) temporal 
phenomic data can reveal a time-dependent association between genotypes and abiotic stresses, which can help 
understand mechanisms to develop resilient plants.
Keywords:   Genomic prediction, high-throughput genotyping, high-throughput phenotyping, phenomic prediction, plant 
breeding (corn), time-dependent association.

Introduction
Genetic gain relies on selection intensity, selection accuracy, 
genetic variance, and the interplay within cycles over time 
(Heffner et al., 2009). These parameters have direct relation-
ships with both the quality and quantity of phenotype data. 
Increasing selection intensity and selection accuracy requires 
growing large numbers of genetically diverse materials and 
collecting phenotypic data from each genotype across cycles. 
Traditional phenotyping approaches provide limited data that 
are resource intensive and scale poorly across time. This results 
in increased uncertainty and error-prone decision-making 
with low accuracy in downstream statistical analysis (Furbank 
and Tester, 2011; Desta and Ortiz, 2014; Araus et al., 2018). 
Field-based high-throughput phenotyping (FHTP) tech-
nology can supply phenomic data that have high time-dimen-
sional (multiple time points) and high organismal resolution, 
despite increasing computational demands and processing. 
FHTP is more affordable per observation and less arduous 
compared with traditional phenotyping methodologies (Shi 
et al., 2016). Using unmanned (aka unoccupied or uncrewed) 
aerial vehicles (UAVs, aka drones) equipped with high-tech 
sensors, plant nurseries and trials can be screened multiple 
times with high spatial (pixel) and temporal (days) resolution 
and at different plant growth stages. Reflectance bands cap-
tured can be used as novel predictors and/or secondary traits 
in prediction of agronomically important traits, such as yield 
and flowering time (Rutkoski et al., 2016; Aguate et al., 2017; 
Wu et al., 2019; Galán et al., 2020; Adak et al., 2021c; Sandhu 
et al., 2021). Extracting reflectance band data from remotely 
sensed images over multiple time points reveals novel tem-
poral phenotypic values for diverse genotypes that can pro-
vide key information for plant scientists to discover critical 
time–trait combinations. This also allows early selection cri-
teria to be proposed, before traditional end-of-season pheno-
types, such as grain yield, have been collected (Sun et al., 2019; 
Krause et al., 2020; Adak et al., 2021c), similar to genomic-
based approaches.
Complex quantitative traits (e.g. yield, plant height, and 
flowering times) generally follow an omnigenic model, where 
thousands of small interacting loci contribute to the forma-
tion of the phenotype (Boyle et al., 2017). To include omnige-
nic contributions of genome-wide markers in selection, they 
can be used as predictors of genetic merit for the phenotypic 
formation of complex traits; this method was first applied in 
maize as genomic prediction (GP) by Bernardo (1994), also 
known as genomic best linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP) 
today. Subsequently, Bayesian GP methods (Meuwissen et al., 
2001) and ridge-regression BLUP (rrBLUP) (Whittaker et al., 
2000; Endelman, 2011) were developed. Prediction results of 
these models across genome-wide markers are now applied 
to the selection of candidate plants known as genomic selec-
tion (GS) (Bernardo and Yu, 2007). GS has proved reliable for 
the selection of complex traits in various crops during plant 
breeding cycles (Heslot et al., 2015; Windhausen et al., 2012; 
Zhao et al., 2012; Crossa et al., 2014). However, GS requires 
significant effort in DNA extractions and genotyping to obtain 
useful genome-wide markers from populations with GP mod-
els. The GS model must be trained for hundreds to thousands 
of candidate plants, then the model must be updated again in 
each growing period for selection. To train accurate GS mod-
els, large phenotypic datasets must be collected, a bottleneck 
in prediction. Reducing resources needed and improving the 
throughput of data collection using FHTP technologies is 
critical to (i) associate phenotypic traits with genomic data to 
boost the potential of GS and (ii) use directly as novel predic-
tors, and where a biological basis of these predictors can be 
explored.
FHTP has revealed dynamic associations between genomic 
data and temporal phenotypes of complex traits never be-
fore possible in plants (Bac-Molenaar et al., 2015; Pauli et al., 
2016; Campbell et al., 2017; Feldman et al., 2017; Ward et al., 
2019; Anderson et al., 2020; Rodene et al., 2022; Adak et al., 
2023b). Phenomic data collected from multiple environments 
and/or multiple time points can serve as better predictors than 
genome-wide markers for selecting desired plants in plant 
breeding (Bernardo, 2021). HTP data can enhance the predic-
tion of complex traits in plants, as well as supply complemen-
tary phenomic datasets for genomics to dissect complex traits 
and their differences in sensitivity to the environments during 
growth.
When combined with high-revisit temporal UAV pheno-
typic data, genome-wide markers have had fluctuating pheno-
typic effects sizes in genome-wide association studies (GWAS) 
and quantitative trait locus (QTL) studies during crop growth 
(Anderson et al., 2020; Adak et al., 2021a, 2023b; Wang et al., 
2021; Rodene et al., 2022). This phenomenon demonstrates 
that the same markers can have temporally different fitness 
for both traits and genotypes. Much like using genome-wide 
markers as predictors, temporal phenotype data of different 
sensor-based traits (e.g. vegetation indices) can also be used as 
predictors. The predictive ability can then be compared with 
the predictive ability of genome-wide markers (Rincent et al., 
2018; Adak et al., 2023b).
Genome-wide markers are collected once, and different 
individuals are typically straightforward to compare at the 
same genetic location regardless of when the data are col-
lected. In contrast, phenomic markers are temporally and 
environmentally dependent and it is still not straightforward 
to compare phenomic features collected in different envi-
ronments, at different growth stages, or on different days. 
Temporal phenotypes are characterized by changing envi-
ronmental variables across growth stages, resulting in unique 
trajectories specific to each environment; these unique 
trajectories may also serve as novel predictors of complex 
traits across diverse environments. Close similarity between

This study used recombinant inbred lines (RILs) from the

RILs, grown in irrigated and non-irrigated (drought) trials that 
served as different environments in 2018. Multiple UAV sur-
veys were conducted using red–green–blue (RGB) sensors and 
multispectral sensors in irrigated and drought trials. The 520 
RILs were also genotyped using an Infinium genotyping assay. 
Days to anthesis (DTA), days to silking (DTS), and terminal 
plant height (PHT) were collected from RILs across irrigated 
and drought trials and predicted by phenomic and genomic 
data using the different scenarios. Specifically, our objectives 
were as follows. (i) To predict the flowering times of unob-
served genotypes between irrigated and drought trials using 
temporal phenomic data derived from RGB and multispec-
tral FHTPs, and compare the phenomic prediction accuracies 
across irrigated and drought trials. (ii) To compare prediction 
abilities of different models with combinations of genomic and 
phenomic data. (iii) To integrate temporal phenomic data with 
genomic data via a GWAS.
Materials and methods
Developing recombinant inbred lines and experimental design
Parental maize inbred lines used for constructing the three bi-paren-
tal linkage populations were developed from crosses Ki3×NC356, 
LH82×LAMA, and Tx740×NC356 (Chen, 2016). From 2014 to 2018, 
these populations were advanced to the F7:8 using the single seed descent 
method across College Station (summer nursery) and Weslaco (winter 
nursery), Texas. In total, 238, 178, and 104 RILs were derived from 
Ki3×NC356, LH82×LAMA, and Tx740×NC356, respectively. LH82 
is a temperate adapted line while Ki3 is tropical and NC356, LAMA, 
and Tx740 are tropical genetic background but temperate selected lines. 
The LAMA line [pedigree: (LAMA2002-12-1-B-B-B-B/LAMA2002-
1-5-B-B-B-B)-3-2-B-1-B3-B] parent is expected to be 50% related to 
Tx740 [pedigree: (LAMA2002-12-1-B-B-B)] (Mayfield et al., 2012). All 
plantings were set using ranges (horizontal grids, 0.76 m between plots) 
and rows (lateral grids 3.81 m long per plot) accounting for random-
ized complete block design with two replications. The experimental de-
sign is visually represented in Supplementary Fig. S1. The RILs were 
grown under two different experimental conditions, with and without 
irrigation, and referred to as irrigated and drought trials in this study. 
These trials were two different managements at the same location in 
the same year, but for ease of presentation we use the term environment 
to separate them here. RILs were planted on 14 March 2018 (irrigated 
and drought trials). Flowering time includes both anthesis and silking in 
maize (Colasanti and Muszynski, 2009). DTA and DTS were recorded 
for each row plot across trials; DTA and DTS were recorded as the time 
from planting to when at least 50% of a plot initiated anthesis and silk-
ing, respectively. PHT was measured manually from the ground to the 
tip of the tassel in each row plot at the end of the reproductive stage; 
flowering times and plant heights were recorded in Field book (Rife 
and Poland, 2014).
Field-based high-throughput phenotyping platforms and image 
processing
Two different UAV platforms were used in this study. A rotary wing 
type UAV (DJI Phantom 3 Professional) equipped with an RGB sensor 
(12 megapixel DJI FC300X camera) was flown at an altitude of 25 
m resulting in ~1  cm per pixel resolution. Additionally, a fixed-wing 
Tuffwing UAV mapper equipped with a multispectral camera (MicaSense 
RedEdge-MX) was flown at an altitude of 120 m resulting in ~7.5 cm 
per pixel resolution. In both platforms, 80% side and forward overlap was 
used. The RGB platform was flown 16 times over irrigated and drought 
trials. Dates of the flights belonging to both platforms with corresponding 
days after planting times are shown in Fig. 1.
To obtain the orthomosaics for every flight, raw images belonging to 
each flight were processed in Pix4Dmapper (https://www.pix4d.com) 
for RGB, and Agisoft PhotoScan (Agisoft PhotoScan Professional v. 1.2.

Data extraction from orthomosaics
A shapefile was first created in QGIS containing the examined pop-
ulation field in each orthomosaic; each orthomosaic was clipped into 
population levels in QGIS (https://qgis.org/en/site) using the ‘Clip 
Raster by Mask Layer’ function, and further data extraction was con-
ducted in R. UAStools (https://github.com/andersst91/UAStools, 
Anderson and Murray, 2020) and FIELDimageR (https://github.com/
OpenDroneMap/FIELDimageR, Matias et al., 2020) packages were 
used in R to create the shape files and extract the vegetation indices 
(VIs), respectively. Briefly, (i) shape files for each plot were created using 
the field map, where range, row, and plot information are stored, using 
UAStools::plotshpcreate(); (ii) high-resolution orthomosaics (only in the 
RGB FHTP platform) were reduced using the raster::aggregate(); (iii) 
FIELDimageR::fieldmask() was used to remove the soil from the vegeta-
tion; (iv) FIELDiamgeR::fieldindex() was used to define the VIs; and (v) 
FIELDimageR::fieldInfo() was used to extract the defined VIs for each 
plot according to the shapefile created in step (i). More complete descrip-
tions of these steps can be found in (Adak et al., 2023b). VIs used in this 
study are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

DNA extraction, genotyping, and filtering
DNA procedures were previously documented in Chen (2016) but are 
briefly described here. Genomic DNA samples extracted from eight seed-
lings were bulked for each RIL using a CTAB (cetyltrimethylammonium 
bromide) protocol (Chen and Ronald, 1999). Genomic DNA for each 
of the 520 genotypes was collected using an Infinium whole-genome 
genotyping assay in AgReliant Genetics LLC; 515 were successfully geno-
typed, resulting in 17 444 genome-wide single nucleotide polymorphisms 
(SNPs). B73 RefGEN_v3 was used to determine the physical location of 
the genome-wide SNPs (Schnable et al., 2009). Tassel 5 software (Bradbury 
et al., 2007) was used to filter and impute the genomic data as follows: (i) 
heterozygote calls set as missing; (ii) markers filtered if allele frequency 
was <0.05 and missing values were >10% across RILs; and, finally (iii) ge-
nomic data were imputed based on LD-kNNi (Money et al., 2015). As a 
result, 11 334 SNPs were used in GWAS and genomic prediction.

To assess the models, prediction ability was calculated via Pearson’s 
correlation between actual values and predicted values of RILs based on 
four different scenarios using 5-fold cross-validation with 25 replications; 
one replication of 5-fold cross-validation is illustrated in Supplementary 
Fig. S2 and explained as follows (Burgueño et al., 2012). Four prediction 
scenarios (CV1, CV2, CV0, and CV00) relevant to plant breeding pro-
grams were applied to each of the seven prediction models. The tested 
RILs were randomly selected RILs that were used in the training of the 
models (4-fold=412 RILs), and the untested RILs were those excluded 
from the models (1-fold=103 RILs).
For CV1 and CV2, models were trained using the tested RILs in 
both irrigated and drought trials. Here irrigated and drought trials were 
considered observed environments together, since predicted (DTA, DTS, 
and PHT) and predictor variables (phenomic and genomic data) of ran-
domly selected tested RILs from both irrigated and drought trials were 
used to train models. Then, both the tested and untested RILs were pre-
dicted, and the two types of correlations for each model were calculated 
as follows: the correlation between the predicted and actual values of the 
untested RILs, called the CV1 scenario, and the correlation between the 
predicted and actual values of the tested RILs, called the CV2 scenario.
For CV0 and CV00, models were trained using the tested RILs in 
only the irrigated trial. Here irrigated and drought trials were consid-
ered the observed and unobserved environments, respectively, as pre-
dicted (DTA, DTS, and PHT) and predictor variables (phenomic and 
genomic data) of randomly selected tested RILs (4-fold=412 RILs) 
from only the irrigated trial were used to train models. Then, both the 
tested and untested RILs were predicted, and the two types of correla-
tions for each model were calculated as follows: the correlation between 
the predicted and actual values of the tested RILs (the same 412 RILs 
trained in the irrigated trial) in the drought trial was called the CV0 
scenario. The correlation between the predicted and actual values of the 
untested RILs (the remaining 103 RILs) in the drought trial was called 
the CV00 scenario. To sum up, CV1 indicates the prediction of untested 
RILs in an observed environment; CV2 indicates the prediction of 
tested RILs in an observed environment; CV0 indicated the prediction 
of tested RILs in an unobserved environment; and CV00 indicates the 
prediction of untested RILs in an unobserved environment. Prediction 
was performed from favorable to unfavorable environments in CV0 and 
CV00 scenarios.

Results
Interaction design results: temporal repeatability, 
variance components, temporal breeding values, and 
correlation
The RGB platform had higher ground resolution (~1 cm per 
pixel) than the multispectral platform (~7.5  cm per pixel), 
indicating that RGB had greater resolution. Among the major 
causes was a lower flight altitude (25 m) of the RGB platform 
than the multispectral HTP platform. This resulted in overall 
higher heritability values in RGB phenomic data. Heritability 
of VIs in multispectral phenomic data varied between ~0.10 
and 0.85 in the irrigated trial, and ~0.13 and 0.86 in the 
drought trial, whereas heritability of VIs in RGB phenomic 
data varied between ~0.84 and 0.93 in the drought trial, and 
~0.86 and 0.92 in the irrigated trial (Fig. 3). The flight com-
ponent in the interaction design (Equation 1) explained the 
highest total variation for most VIs in the RGB phenomic 
dataset (Fig. 3). Results of interaction terms [(FG)(i×j) in 
Equation 1], in which genetic materials (RILs) were consid-
ered to have interactions across flights, were used to predict 
temporal breeding values of RILs for each VI in RGB and 
multispectral phenomic data. Correlations between individu-
als VIs separately in drought and irrigated phenomic data and 
flowering time and plant height were found to change tem-
porally between –0.4 and +0.4 depending on flight dates. 
Temporal RGB VIs had a greater magnitude of correlations in 
flowering times from positive to negative than multispectral 
VIs (Fig. 4).
Phenomic data correlations showed that VIs belonging to 
the same growth stages were usually correlated with flow-
ering and plant height in a similar way (Fig. 4). Temporal data 
of VIs had minimal multicollinearity in the phenomic dataset 
(Fig. 5).
Differences in flowering times and plant height 
between different environments
The genotype by environment model (Equation 3) revealed 
differences in flowering times and plant heights across irri-
gated and drought trials. Interaction variance components 
between genotype (RILs) and environments explained ~8% 
of total variation for DTA and DTS, and 19% for plant 
height (Fig. 6). The changes in flowering times and plant 
height across different environments were used as dependent 
predicted variables to assess predictive ability of genomic, 
phenomic, and combined data in applying four different pre-
diction scenarios.

Overall, these results indicate that VIs derived from remotely 
sensed images can capture known variation in flowering time 
and discover flowering-related loci in association mapping, 
in most cases even earlier than using actual flowering times. 
Because temporal values of VIs are capable of detecting flow-
ering time variation even before flowering, their early and 
temporal assessment of complex traits could help decipher the 
underlying causal biology and growth modeling (Anderson 
et al., 2019).
Any large phenomic dataset raises concerns about multi-
collinearity, redundancy in predictors, and potential resulting 
problems, especially model overfitting (Adak et al., 2021c). The 
temporal phenomic and genomic data were subjected to corre-
lation calculations to assess multicollinearity, both ranging from 
–1 to 1 (Fig. 5). We found no evidence that multicollinearity 
was any more present in temporal phenomic data than in ge-
nomic data (Fig. 5), despite having a relatively sparse genomic 
marker set compared with many modern studies. This suggests 
that if the scientific community is not concerned about multi-
collinearity in genomics, there should be even fewer concerns 
in phenomics at this resolution. Regardless, modern machine 
learning minimizes the major concerns around multicollinear-
ity analysis (Montesinos López et al., 2022). Both the phenomic 
and genomic measures remained unsaturated in this study, and 
more measures (more features in phenomics, more SNPs in ge-
nomics) could prove beneficial to improve predictions.
Among phenomic data, the majority of observed multicol-
linearity was between some of the many VIs calculated within 
a given time point. However, it remained surprising how few 
multicollinearity issues there were given the limited number 
of spectral bands used to calculate VIs. This suggests that there 
might be additional VIs that could be utilized to improve predic-
tion. Essentially no additional resources are needed to calculate 
additional VI measures within a UAV time point, so it is logical 
to extract as many VI features as possible from each orthomosaic, 
even if some multicollinearity is observed. More noteworthy was 
the low multicollinearity across the relatively high temporal di-
mension (multiple flight times near to each other). This suggests 
that measurable and unique biological variation occurs within 
very short windows of time. A higher temporal dimension and 
phenomic features could be used to both improve prediction 
and to better understand plant development. Various FHTP-
related predictions reported inconsistency across different years 
and environments (Rutkoski et al., 2016; Crain et al., 2018) and 
this may stem from using low number of phenomic feature (VIs) 
or low temporal flight resolution. Conducting additional UAV 
flights requires resources proportional to the number of flights, 
so finding the optimal number and times to collect data remains 
an important open question in FHTP.
Temporal phenomic data used as predictors in prediction 
models generated promising prediction accuracies between 
diverse trials. Phenomic tools have appeared to be as good or 
better than genomic tools (Rincent et al., 2018; Adak et al., 
2023a). Similarly, we have found that RGB phenomic data 
predicted flowering times and plant height better than ge-
nomic data (M1 versus M3 in CV1, Fig. 7). However, we also 
found that combined data (phenomic plus genomic data) 
with interactions with environment yielded greater predictive 
ability than using genomic or phenomic data alone for flow-
ering time and plant height in maize (M4 to M7 in Fig. 7). 
Thus, temporal phenomic data add complementary predictive 
information for flowering time and perhaps other complex 
traits in maize.
Temporal phenomic prediction of complex traits
In previous studies, agronomic complex traits (e.g. flow-
ering time and yield) were predicted from one or a limited 
number of VIs belonging to one or a limited number of time 
points and in one environment. For example, BNDVI (nor-
malized difference between the blue and near infrared wave-
length bands) was used as a predictor to predict grain yield 
and flowering time in maize using the PLS regression; the 
prediction accuracies (0.54–0.79) varied depending on the 
temporal resolution (number of flights) (Wu et al., 2019). 
Prediction accuracies for flowering times as well as grain 
yield increased across diverse environments when a higher 
number of VIs over multiple time points were jointly used 
(Adak et al., 2021c, 2023a). Aguate et al. (2017) evaluated the 
performance of 62 hyperspectral reflectance bands between 
392 nm and 850 nm obtained at 300 m of flight elevation 
in predicting maize yield across diverse scenarios (popula-
tions, small plots, a farmers field, etc.) and over five time 
points. They found that single time point-derived reflectance

Genomic and phenomic prediction
CV1 and CV00 demonstrated that the predictive abilities of 
genome-wide makers decrease when it comes to predicting 
unknown genotypes in known and unknown environments 
(Fig. 7). This is in part because differences in flowering time 
driven by different environments cannot easily be taken into 
consideration by genome-wide markers, since genomic varia-
tion remains fixed across all environments (Jarquín et al., 2014; 
Cooper et al., 2016). It is therefore logical that results of CV1 
showed that prediction of flowering times and plant height of 
unknown genotypes in known environments was better pre-
dicted with high-resolution RGB phenomic data than with 
either genomic or multispectral phenomic data alone (M1 
versus M2 and M3 in CV1; Fig. 7). Combining phenomic and 
genomic data in prediction better captures the genomic and 
environmental variation together, especially in the most chal-
lenging scenario, namely the prediction of the performance of 
untested genotype in untested environments (CV00).
Boosting prediction ability by combining VIs with genomic 
data has also been shown for yield in wheat and rye (Rutkoski 
et al., 2016; Crain et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2019; Galán et al., 2020). 
Our results support that instead of using single pre-selected VIs 
(as is common) or limited reflectance bands, a higher number 
of VIs may provide greater prediction ability and high consist-
ency across environments without issues of additional cost or 
multicollinearity. It is notable that this is a phenomic parallel to 
a similar transition trend from GWAS and marker-assisted se-
lection, targeting specific markers, to genomic prediction using 
all markers.
Integration of high-throughput phenomic data with 
association mapping and genomic prediction
Temporal plant height (aka, canopy height measurement; CHM) 
captured by UAV has previously been used as phenotype data 
in association mapping in maize hybrids (Adak et al., 2021b, 
2023a) and inbreds (Wang et al., 2019; Anderson et al., 2020), 
resulting in discovery of numerous temporal loci. Temporal 
loci explain phenotypic variation of plant height differently 
depending on the specific growth stage; this allows better bi-
ological understanding of the loci segregating in a population 
that control different stages of growth. Similar findings have 
been shown in soybean using UAV-based phenotyping (Xavier 
et al., 2017), in cotton using ground-based vehicle phenotyp-
ing (Pauli et al., 2016), in wheat, rice, and triticale using trac-
tor-based phenotyping (Würschum et al., 2014; Tanger et al., 
2017; Lyra et al., 2020), as well as in maize and barley using 
automated-based phenotyping under greenhouse conditions 
(Muraya et al., 2017; Neumann et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2017). 
Temporal NDVIs (normalized difference vegetation indexes) 
captured by UAVs have also been used in association studies in 
cotton (Pauli et al., 2016), sorghum (Spindel et al., 2018), and 
maize (Wang et al., 2021). However, the present study showed 
that NDVI discovered among the lowest number of SNPs in 
GWAS mapping (Fig. 8A). This reinforces that using only one 
or a few temporal phenotypes of VIs or CHM will not broaden 
the discovery of segregating temporal loci controlling plant 
growth.
It was intriguing that high-resolution RGB images discov-
ered more loci during mid-growth, around flowering, than 
other growth periods, while low-resolution multispectral 
images discovered loci quite close during and after flowering 
times (Fig. 8B). It seems likely that the high-resolution RGB 
better captured the differences in plant development such as 
leaf area index, and senescence with more plant pixels. It is 
likely that multispectral images better distinguished tassel pix-
els from leaf pixels during flowering than RGB. These results 
showed that temporal phenotypes captured by multiple drone 
surveys and sensors can discover previously unrevealed time-
dependent associations between temporal traits and genome-
wide polymorphisms (Fig. 9; Supplementary Table S2). Overall, 
dissection of temporal plant growth is better if high time-
dimensional phenotype data are used in association mapping 
studies.
Validation of a candidate gene using temporal 
phenotype data
A heat stress-related candidate gene, GRMZM2G083810 
(hsp18f), was discovered using the temporal blue index phe-
notype around only flowering time in both drought and 
irrigated trials. Flowering time is among the most sensitive 
periods to heat and drought stress (Barnabás et al., 2008), and 
environmental heat stress probably led to the discovery of 
GRMZM2G083810 (hsp18f) during flowering (Fig. 10A). This 
finding demonstrates that temporal phenotypes can enhance 
discovering opportunistic (oQTLs) (Wu et al., 2004). High-
resolution HTFP images in particular can capture the specific 
interplays between plant growth and biotic stresses during

certain growth periods. The GRMZM2G083810 (hsp18f) 
candidate gene caused the RIL haplotypes to have statistically 
different flowering as well as terminal plant height (Fig. 10B, 
C) in both drought and irrigated trials. It is also surprising that 
GRMZM2G083810 (hsp18f) was discovered for plant height 
in the drought trial (Fig. 9A). This indicated that the allelic 
variation associated with heat stress-related genes discovered in 
early plant growth stages can impact phenotypes such as ter-
minal plant height and flowering times (Fig. 9B, C). This result 
is a proof of concept that UAVs can provide time-specific tem-
poral phenotype data when plants are exposed to abiotic stress 
factors during growth (e.g. heat stress during flowering times 
in this study). Thus, relationships between plant abiotic stresses 
and candidate genes related to these stresses can be studied in 
greater biological detail with temporal data.
NDRBI was chosen as a model VI to further evaluate, due 
to having the highest ability of any RGB index to detect 
SNPs in the GWAS (Fig. 8A) as well as the highest herita-
bility, lowest error, and highest R2 (Fig. 3). Temporal genomic 
prediction was applied to a temporal value of NDRBI de-
rived from multiple UAV flights during growth to examine 
whether the temporal phenotype is governed by genome-
wide markers similarly or differently during growth and be-
tween environments. The results showed that there was a large 
variation in predicting temporal values of the NDRBI index 
across time points as well as trials in maize (Fig. 11A). The 
main reason is likely to be that the temporal values of the VI 
are influenced by both genetic and environmental factors as 
well as their interactions. This means that the relationship be-
tween the genomic data and the phenotype of interest may 
not be constant across different time periods due to changes 
in environmental conditions. Because the flight component 
explained the highest portion of explained variation in both 
trials, that provides some evidence of the dynamic relationship 
between temporal values of the VI and the environment (Fig. 
11B). It was discovered that temporal NDRBI values belong-
ing to time points between 75 and 99 DAPs were genomi-
cally predicted to be greater than those at 27–71 DAPs in 
both trials (Fig. 11). Among the most likely reasons is that 
the overall range of measurable phenotypic variation is larger 
in adult plants than in seedlings, as well as the range of her-
itable variation attributable to the genetic component. The 
resulting larger signal to measurement noise would be ex-
pected to result in predictions performing better in more ma-
ture plants. Another component might be that as the plant 
matures, the many genetic, environmental, and interaction 
factors that influence the phenotype of interest accumulate, 
making accumulated differences between genotypes more 
stable and predictable. In addition to these factors, the genetic 
architecture of the trait of interest may also change as the plant 
matures. For example, it is recognized that expression of cer-
tain genes may be turned on or off at different growth stages, 
thus the relative importance of different genetic markers for 
these genes may shift as the trait is expressed differentially over 
time (Zhou et al., 2019). Overall, these findings demonstrated 
the variability in the prediction of temporal phenotype data 
under diverse environments since genomic markers had dy-
namic phenotypic effect sizes across different growth stages 
and environments (Fig. 12). Repetitive UAV surveys can be 
used to evaluate temporal prediction abilities of genome-wide 
markers for specific time points and environments. This ap-
proach advises using temporal values of agronomically im-
portant VIs in genomic prediction to better understand the 
predictability of genome-wide markers across times points 
and environments beyond using only one-time terminal phe-
notypic values (DTA, DTS, and PHT) as dependent variables.
Conclusion
FHTP incorporates novel technologies and applications such 
as UAVs and imaging sensors that can screen segregating popu-
lations with high precision, multiple measures, and a high time 
dimension. These high dimensional data allowed prediction 
and dissection of novel interactions between environment, 
plant, and underlying segregating genomic mechanisms. First, 
temporal phenomic spectral data belonging to multiple time 
points throughout growth predicted complex phenological 
traits (e.g. flowering time and plant height) across different 
environments, as well as helping to understand the stability 
of flowering. Second, temporal phenotype data were found 
to better dissect time-dependent associations between plant 
and abiotic stress. Finally, temporal reflectance bands led to the 
discovery of many loci, including known genes and a heat-
related candidate gene only when heat stress reached a peak 
around flowering. Overall, temporal phenomic prediction and 
dissecting time-dependent association between temporal phe-
notype, genomic, and environmental data were demonstrated. 
Integration of high-throughput phenomic data into plant 
breeding and genomics will allow biological understanding to 
select novel complex traits and dissect the plant–environment 
interaction across plant development.
